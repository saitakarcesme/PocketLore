Pickling and food drying are both methods used to inhibit spoilage, but they do so through different mechanisms involving the role of water. Pickling solutions are typically highly acidic, with a pH of 4.6 or lower, and high in salt, which prevents enzymes from working and microorganisms from multiplying [[S1]]. The acidity and salinity of the solution, along with the temperature of fermentation and the exclusion of oxygen, determine which microorganisms dominate and the flavor of the end product. In pickling, water plays a role in the process by being drawn out of the food through salting, as in the case of sour cabbage, sauerkraut, and kimchi, where excess water is removed to create a brine that facilitates fermentation by lactic acid bacteria [[S1]].

Food drying, on the other hand, inhibits the growth of bacteria, yeasts, and mold through the removal of water [[S2]]. The process of drying involves the traditional removal of water through evaporation using methods such as air drying, sun drying, smoking, or wind drying, and modern methods like electric food dehydrators or freeze-drying. The removal of water is crucial in food drying as it creates an environment that is inhospitable to microorganisms, thus preserving the food. In this method, water is the key factor in inhibiting spoilage, as the absence of water prevents the growth of spoilage organisms.
